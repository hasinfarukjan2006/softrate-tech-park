GST - Goods and Services Tax

The Goods and Services Tax (GST) is a comprehensive indirect tax introduced in India to replace multiple central and state taxes. GST was implemented on July 1, 2017, with the objective of creating a unified tax structure across the country. It applies to the supply of goods and services and helps eliminate the cascading effect of taxation.

GST improves transparency, simplifies tax compliance, and enables businesses to operate under a single tax framework. Different categories of goods and services are taxed at various GST rates such as 0%, 5%, 12%, 18%, and 28%.

More on GST

GST is one of the most significant tax reforms in India. It simplifies indirect taxation by combining several taxes into one unified system. Businesses benefit from easier compliance, improved tax credit mechanisms, and streamlined reporting requirements.

GST rates vary depending on the nature of goods and services. Essential items may attract lower tax rates while luxury products and selected services may fall under higher GST slabs.

There are four types of GST active in India. They are:

CGST
Central Goods and Services Tax

CGST is levied by the Central Government on intra-state supplies of goods and services. Revenue collected under CGST is retained by the Central Government.

SGST
State Goods and Services Tax

SGST is levied by the State Government on intra-state supplies. Revenue collected under SGST belongs to the respective state government.

IGST
Integrated Goods and Services Tax

IGST is applicable on inter-state transactions and imports. Revenue collected under IGST is shared between the Central Government and the destination state.

UTGST
Union Territory Goods and Services Tax

UTGST is applicable in Union Territories where no legislature exists. It is collected alongside CGST.

    What is a barcode?

    A barcode is a machine-readable representation of data in a visual format on a surface. Barcodes represent data by varying the widths and spacings of parallel lines, commonly referred to as linear or one-dimensional (1D) barcodes. Scanners and camera sensors can read these patterns, translating them into SKU codes, prices, and tracking data in an instant.

    Benefits of using barcodes

    Easy product identification

    Ensure instant lookups of item descriptions, SKUs, and specifications without manual typing errors.

    Faster billing

    Speed up sales and checkout pipelines by scanning product barcodes directly into invoice forms.

    Inventory tracking

    Track stock levels, monitor warehouse movements, and audit stock levels with minimal human effort.

    Types of barcodes

    CODE128

    High-density, highly compact alphanumeric barcode format. Accepts letters, numbers, and punctuation. Ideal for logistics, supply chain, and retail tags.

    EAN13

    International standard for retail product labeling. Requires 12 numeric digits plus a checksum digit (13 digits total) to route and log items in retail systems.

    UPC

    Universal Product Code format used widely in North America. Consists of 11 numeric digits plus a checksum digit (12 digits total) to track retail goods.

    CODE39

    Alphanumeric barcode format that supports uppercase letters, numbers, and standard symbols. Widely used in automotive, electronics, and government inventories.

    What is Economic Order Quantity (EOQ)?

    Economic Order Quantity (EOQ) is the optimal order quantity that a company should purchase to minimize its total inventory costs, such as holding costs, shortage costs, and order costs. This financial model helps businesses manage their cash flow, reduce warehousing expenses, and ensure supply chain efficiency.

    EOQ Formula

    The standard EOQ formula uses three inputs (Annual Demand, Ordering Cost, and Holding Cost) to find the cost-minimizing quantity:

    • EOQ Formula: \(EOQ = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times D \times S}{H}}\)
    • Where:
      • D (Annual Demand): The total volume of units demanded per year.
      • S (Ordering Cost): The cost incurred per purchase order (setup cost).
      • H (Holding Cost): The annual carrying or holding cost per unit (storage, insurance, etc.).

    Benefits of EOQ

    Implementing the EOQ model provides several key benefits for inventory managers:

    • Minimizes Storage Costs: Prevents holding unnecessary inventory, directly reducing rent and warehousing costs.
    • Reduces Order Admin Expenses: Balances order frequencies, limiting shipping fees and administrative setup overheads.
    • Limits Obsolescence: Prevents shelf-spoilage and depreciation of inventory items.

    How EOQ Works & Examples

    Suppose a business has an annual demand of 10,000 units, an ordering cost of ₹50 per order, and a holding cost of ₹2 per unit per year.

    Substituting these values into the formula: \(EOQ = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 10,000 \times 50}{2}} = \sqrt{500,000} \approx \mathbf{708\text{ units}}\).

    At this optimal quantity, ordering costs and holding costs are balanced, ensuring minimum overall inventory costs.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    The standard EOQ model assumes that demand, ordering costs, and holding costs are completely constant throughout the year. It does not account for volume discounts, seasonal variations, or sudden spikes in market demand.

    Holding costs are inversely proportional to EOQ. A higher holding cost makes it expensive to store products, prompting the model to recommend ordering smaller quantities more frequently to reduce average warehouse volume.

    What is the Break-Even Point?

    The break-even point is the milestone where total revenues equal total expenses. At this specific point, your business operates at a net profit of zero: you have covered all of your operating liabilities but have not yet generated a surplus. Knowing your break-even point is critical for business planning, pricing optimization, and calculating your margin of safety.

    How is the Break-Even Point Calculated?

    Our calculator computes profitability targets based on the following standard corporate financial formulas:

    • Contribution Margin = Selling Price per Unit - Variable Cost per Unit
    • Break-Even Point (Units) = Fixed Costs ÷ Contribution Margin
    • Break-Even Point (Revenue) = Break-Even Point (Units) × Selling Price per Unit

    Frequently Asked Questions

    Fixed costs are consistent overheads that do not vary with output or sales, such as rent, salaries, and insurance. Variable costs are directly tied to production levels, such as raw materials, packaging, and direct sales commissions.

    The contribution margin ratio is the percentage of sales revenue that exceeds variable costs. It represents the proportion of each sales dollar available to cover fixed expenses and generate operating profit.

    A lower break-even point reduces operating leverage risk. It means the company requires fewer sales units to avoid operating losses, which provides a higher margin of safety during market downturns.

    What is a reorder point?

    A reorder point is a threshold that helps you maintain the right inventory level. It's a tool for keeping tabs on the inventory level of your items so that you do not run out of stock. Whenever the item count reaches the reorder point, you get automatically notified and you know it is the right time to create a purchase order with your supplier.     What is a Stock Keeping Unit (SKU)?

    An SKU is an internal product code composed of letters and numbers that identifies a product's specific characteristics (like brand, model, size, color). Unlike UPC barcodes, SKUs are unique to your business and are designed to be easily read by humans.

    How should I structure my SKU codes?

    A good SKU structure flows from broad to specific characteristics. For example, start with the category abbreviation (e.g. ELEC), followed by brand (SOF), product attributes (BLK-M), and finally a sequential item number (001). This forms ELEC-SOF-BLK-M-001.

    What are the best practices for SKU management?

    1. Keep codes short (typically 8-12 characters).
    2. Avoid using spaces, slashes, or special symbols that can break database queries (hyphens or underscores are best).
    3. Do not start SKUs with zero, as spreadsheet software may delete it.
    4. Avoid using letters that look like numbers (such as O, I, or L).

    Are SKUs and UPC barcodes the same?

    No. SKUs are internal codes unique to each business, while UPC (Universal Product Code) barcodes are external, standardized 12-digit numbers issued by GS1 that remain identical across all retail stores globally.

    What is UK VAT?

    Value Added Tax (VAT) is a consumption tax placed on a product whenever value is added at each stage of the supply chain, from production to the point of sale. For businesses registered in the UK, VAT is charged on most goods and services.

    Current VAT Rates in the UK

    The UK has three main VAT categories:

    • Standard Rate (20%): Applies to most goods and services.
    • Reduced Rate (5%): Applies to some goods and services, such as home energy, children's car seats, and certain renovations.
    • Zero Rate (0%): Applies to zero-rated goods and services, such as most food items, books, children's clothes, and public transport.

    How is VAT calculated?

    To add VAT to an amount, multiply the net amount by 1 plus the VAT rate (expressed as a decimal). For example, to add 20% VAT to £100: £100 × 1.20 = £120.

    To remove VAT from a gross amount, divide the gross amount by 1 plus the VAT rate (expressed as a decimal). For example, to remove 20% VAT from £120: £120 / 1.20 = £100. The VAT amount is the difference: £120 - £100 = £20.

    What is UAE VAT?

    Value Added Tax (VAT) was introduced in the United Arab Emirates on 1 January 2018. It is an indirect tax applied on most goods and services at each stage of the supply chain, collected by registered businesses on behalf of the Federal Tax Authority (FTA).

    Current VAT Rates in the UAE

    The UAE VAT system includes the following rates:

    • Standard Rate (5%): Applies to most goods and services, including food, commercial buildings, hotel services, and utilities.
    • Zero Rate (0%): Applies to specific sectors such as international transport of passengers and goods, supply of certain educational and healthcare services, and first supply of residential buildings.
    • Exempt: Certain financial services, residential buildings (other than the first supply), bare land, and local passenger transport are exempt from VAT.

    What is the UK Flat Rate VAT Scheme?

    The Flat Rate Scheme is designed to simplify VAT accounting for small businesses in the UK. Instead of recording and claiming VAT on every single purchase, you pay a fixed percentage of your gross turnover (including VAT) to HMRC, and you keep the difference between what you charge customers and what you pay to HMRC.

    You cannot reclaim VAT on purchases (except for single capital assets costing £2,000 or more).

    Eligibility criteria

    You can join the scheme if your VAT-taxable turnover (excluding VAT) is £150,000 or less in the next 12 months. You must leave the scheme if your total turnover (including VAT) exceeds £230,000 in a 12-month period.

    What is a Limited Cost Trader?

    If your business spends less than 2% of its turnover (or less than £250 per quarter / £1,000 per year) on "relevant goods" (raw materials, physical goods, but excluding office equipment, gas/electricity, services, vehicle costs, and food/drink), you are classified as a Limited Cost Trader. In this case, you must pay a higher flat rate of 16.5%, regardless of your sector's standard flat rate.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    A tax invoice is the invoice created by a GST registered business owner when he sells taxable goods and services. Tax invoices are mandatory for claiming Input Tax Credit.

    A bill of supply is issued instead of a tax invoice by registered taxpayers who are either selling exempt goods/services or are registered under the GST Composition Scheme. No tax is charged on a bill of supply.

    A proforma invoice is a draft or preliminary invoice sent to buyers before shipment or delivery. It specifies the description and quantity of goods, value, and terms of sale, but does not carry tax obligations or serve as a payment demand.

    GSTIN is a 15-digit unique Goods and Services Tax Identification Number. You can obtain one by registering online through the official GST Common Portal (gst.gov.in) with valid proof of identity and place of business.

    Intrastate transactions (within the same state) are charged with CGST (Central GST) and SGST (State GST) split equally. Interstate transactions (between two different states) are charged with IGST (Integrated GST) in full.

    For goods, a tax invoice must be issued before or at the time of removal of goods. For services, the invoice must be issued within 30 days of the completion of service (45 days for banks/financial institutions).

    For supply of goods, invoices must be prepared in triplicate (Original for Recipient, Duplicate for Transporter, Triplicate for Supplier). For supply of services, invoices must be prepared in duplicate (Original and Duplicate).

    HSN (Harmonised System of Nomenclature) is an international classification code for goods. SAC (Service Accounting Code) is a classification system for services under GST.

    Place of Supply determines whether a transaction is interstate or intrastate. It is generally the location where goods are delivered or where services are performed.

    Reverse charge is a mechanism under GST where the liability to pay tax is on the buyer or recipient instead of the seller. It applies to specific goods/services and purchases from unregistered dealers.

    Composite supply refers to supplying two or more naturally bundled goods/services together (e.g., laptop with charger). Mixed supply refers to supplying goods/services together that are not naturally bundled (e.g., gift pack of chocolates, canned juices, and cakes).

    Input Tax Credit allows a business to reduce the tax it has already paid on inputs (purchases) from the tax it collects on outputs (sales), preventing double taxation.

    What is HRA Exemption?

    House Rent Allowance (HRA) is an important component of the salary structure for salaried individuals living in rented accommodations. Section 10(13A) of the Income Tax Act provides tax relief on the rent paid, allowing employees to claim deduction and lower their taxable income.

    Metro vs Non-Metro

    For the purpose of HRA tax calculation, cities are classified into two categories:

    • Metro Cities: Delhi, Mumbai, Kolkata, and Chennai. Eligible limit is 50% of basic salary.
    • Non-Metro Cities: All other cities in India. Eligible limit is 40% of basic salary.

    Key Prerequisites

    • You must be a salaried employee receiving HRA as part of your salary.
    • You must reside in a rented house and pay rent for it.
    • The rent paid must exceed 10% of your basic salary.

    HRA Exemption Details & FAQ

    How HRA Tax Exemption is Determined

    Tax exemption on HRA is not flat. It is calculated by taking the lowest of three amounts:

    1. The actual House Rent Allowance received from your employer.
    2. The actual rent paid minus 10% of your Basic Salary + Dearness Allowance (DA).
    3. 50% of the Basic Salary (+ DA) if you reside in a metro city, or 40% if you live in a non-metro city.

    Required Documents to Claim HRA

    To successfully claim HRA tax deductions from your employer or during ITR filing, keep these ready:

    • Rent Receipts: Signed rent receipts for the financial year.
    • Rent Agreement: Valid registered rent agreement with your landlord.
    • Landlord's PAN: If the annual rent paid exceeds ₹1,00,000, you must submit the landlord's PAN.

    What is EPS?

    The Employees' Pension Scheme (EPS) is a social security scheme offered by the Employees' Provident Fund Organisation (EPFO). Launched in 1995, it provides monthly pension benefits to employees working in the organized sector upon reaching their retirement age of 58.

    While employee contributions go entirely towards their Provident Fund (EPF) account, employers contribute 12% of the employee's basic salary + DA. Out of this 12% employer contribution, 8.33% is redirected into the Employees' Pension Scheme (EPS) fund.

    EPS Pension Formula

    For employees drawing their pension benefits upon retirement, the monthly pension amount is computed using the following statutory formula:

    Monthly Pension = (Pensionable Salary × Pensionable Service) ÷ 70

    Pensionable Salary: This is the average of the employee's monthly salary (Basic + DA) drawn during the last 60 months of contribution. Under default rules, this salary is capped at ₹15,000 per month, unless the employee has opted for the Higher Pension Scheme based on actual wages.

    Pensionable Service: The total service period of the employee. If the additional months of service are 6 months or more, it is rounded up to the next full year (e.g., 9 years 7 months is counted as 10 years; 9 years 5 months is counted as 9 years).

    Eligibility Criteria

    To qualify for monthly pension benefits under the EPS scheme, an employee must fulfill the following statutory conditions:

    • Must be an active member of the Employees' Provident Fund Organisation (EPFO).
    • Must have completed a minimum of 10 years of continuous pensionable service.
    • Must reach the age of 58 to receive the full regular pension benefit. Alternatively, reduced early pension can be claimed starting at age 50.

    EPS Benefits

    • Superannuation Pension: Regular monthly pension payable to members who retire after reaching the age of 58 with at least 10 years of pensionable service.
    • Early Pension: Members who have completed 10 years of service but have not reached 58 can opt for early pension from age 50 onwards (reduced by 3% for each year below 58).
    • Orphan/Widow Pension: In the unfortunate event of the member's death, the monthly pension is payable to the surviving spouse and children.
    • Withdrawal Benefit: Members who exit service before completing 10 years of pensionable service can withdraw their accumulated EPS corpus.

    Examples

    Let's consider two examples to understand how service years are rounded and how the pension is calculated:

    • Example 1 (Months ≥ 6):
      Pensionable Salary: ₹15,000
      Service Duration: 9 years and 7 months
      Service years considered: 10 years (rounded up since additional months ≥ 6)
      Monthly Pension: (₹15,000 × 10) ÷ 70 = ₹2,142.86
    • Example 2 (Months < 6):
      Pensionable Salary: ₹15,000
      Service Duration: 9 years and 5 months
      Service years considered: 9 years (rounded down since additional months < 6)
      Monthly Pension: (₹15,000 × 9) ÷ 70 = ₹1,928.57

    What is NPS?

    NPS stands for National Pension Scheme. It is a voluntary social security retirement scheme launched by the Government of India. Designed to help individuals accumulate a retirement corpus, NPS allows subscribers to invest regularly throughout their working life, building a secure financial foundation post-retirement.

    Anyone between the ages of 18 and 60 (extended to 70 under revised guidelines) who complies with KYC requirements is eligible to join the NPS.

    Benefits of NPS

    • Retirement Security: Accumulate a robust financial corpus for life after retirement.
    • Tax Efficiency: Claim deductions under Section 80C, Section 80CCD(1B), and enjoy tax-free withdrawals at maturity.
    • Flexible Options: Choose between active choice or auto choice asset allocations (Equities, Corporate Bonds, Government Securities, and Alternative Assets).
    • Compound Growth: Benefit from long-term compound growth under professional pension fund management.

    How NPS Works

    Subscribers make regular contributions to their Tier 1 NPS account during their working years. These contributions are allocated to equity, debt, and government instruments. At age 60, the subscriber can withdraw up to 60% of the accumulated corpus completely tax-free as a lump sum. The remaining 40% (minimum) must be used to purchase an annuity plan from an approved insurance company to receive a regular monthly pension.

    NPS Calculation Formula

    The NPS retirement corpus accumulates via compound growth on monthly contributions. The mathematical formula for computing the maturity value (Annuity Due) is:

    A = P × (1 + r) × [((1 + r)n - 1) / r]

    Where:

    • A: Total accumulated corpus at maturity
    • P: Monthly contribution amount
    • r: Monthly interest rate (annual expected return rate ÷ 12 ÷ 100)
    • n: Total number of months (contribution period in years × 12)

    Tax Benefits Under NPS

    • Section 80C: Contributions are tax-deductible up to ₹1.5 lakh per financial year.
    • Section 80CCD(1B): Claim an additional exclusive tax deduction of up to ₹50,000, over and above the Section 80C limit.
    • Section 10(12A): The lump sum withdrawal at retirement (up to 60% of the total corpus) is completely tax-free.

    NPS Withdrawal Rules

    • Upon Maturity (Age 60): Up to 60% of the total accumulated corpus can be withdrawn as tax-free lump sum. Minimum 40% must be used to purchase an annuity.
    • Premature Exit (Before Age 60): Minimum 80% of the corpus must be used to purchase an annuity if the total corpus exceeds ₹2.5 lakh. If it is under ₹2.5 lakh, full withdrawal is allowed.
    • Partial Withdrawals: Up to 25% of self-contributions can be withdrawn after 3 years of membership for specific purposes (education, medical, marriage, home construction).

    Examples

    Example: Consider Albert, a 25-year-old employee, who contributes ₹3,000 every month to his NPS account. He plans to retire at age 60 (a total contribution period of 35 years), expecting an average annual return of 10%.

    • Total Investment: ₹3,000 × 35 years × 12 months = ₹12,60,000
    • Interest Earned: ₹1,02,24,830
    • Maturity Amount (Corpus): ₹1,14,84,830
    • Annuity Purchase (40%): ₹45,93,932 (used to generate monthly pension)
    • Lump Sum Withdrawal (60%): ₹68,90,898 (tax-free lump sum)
    • Expected Monthly Pension: ₹45,93,932 × 10% expected return / 12 = ₹38,283 (assuming 10% annuity return for demonstration)

    What is a Payslip?

    A salary slip or payslip is an official document containing a detailed list of the various components of your salary along with the specific details of employment. It is issued every month by an employer either in the form of a printed hard copy or an electronic digital copy.

    A salary slip is only available to salaried employees, and your employer is responsible for providing you a copy of your payslip every month as proof of payroll run.

    Why Payslips are Important

    • Income Tax Planning: A salary typically comprises various components such as Basic and HRA which have different tax treatments. Knowing the value of separate components forms the basis for maximizing your tax savings under the applicable financial year.
    • Proof of Employment: This document serves as legal proof of employment. While applying for visas or executive programs at universities, you may have to submit copies of your payslips as proof of your last drawn salary and designation.
    • Availing Loans & Credit Cards: Your payslip contains details of your monthly income, which is a key factor that determines your ability to meet debt obligations. Banks require salary slips when applying for credit cards, loans, mortgages, and other borrowings.
    • Seeking Further Employment: Knowledge about the components of your salary slip helps you assess other job offers. Moreover, it forms the basis of salary negotiations when applying for a new job.

    How to Read a Payslip

    Reading a payslip is easy once you understand its layout. You should verify your company details and employee parameters at the top. The financial section splits into two distinct categories: Earnings (what you receive) and Deductions (what is subtracted for tax or savings). Subtracting the total deductions from your gross earnings results in your Net Pay, which is the final cash amount transferred to your bank account.

    Benefits of Digital Payslips

    Moving from paper payslips to digital copies brings multiple business benefits. Digital files can be stored securely, reducing the risk of administrative loss. It simplifies record-keeping, allows quick document search, and reduces printing costs while helping your organization stay environmentally friendly and reduce its carbon footprint.

    What are the components of a Payslip?

    The two major components of a Payslip are Earnings and Deductions. Toggle between them below to read details.

    Basic Salary This is the primary component of your employee's salary and acts as the foundation upon which other calculations are made (such as PF contribution).
    House Rent Allowance (HRA) An allowance paid to help employees cover their house rent. Depending on the city tier, it ranges between 40% and 50% of the basic salary, and can be claimed for tax exemptions.
    Conveyance Allowance Travel allowance provided to offset commuting costs between home and office.
    Medical Allowance An allowance provided to cover regular medical expenses incurred by the employee.
    Other Allowances Includes performance bonuses, special allowances, and any other additional allowances paid by the employer.
    Provident Fund (PF) A retirement saving scheme. Salaried employees typically have 12% of their basic salary deducted and contributed to their EPF account, which is matched by the employer.
    Professional Tax A state-level tax levied on profession and employment. It is calculated based on state tax slabs and deducted monthly.
    Tax Deducted at Source (TDS / Income Tax) The income tax deducted by the employer from the employee's salary on behalf of the Income Tax department, calculated based on the employee's taxable income slab.
    Other Deductions Any miscellaneous deductions, such as advances, employee welfare fund, or voluntary savings.

    The balance sheet is based on an equation where assets are on one side, liabilities and shareholders' equity are on the other side, and both sides balance out.

    According to the equation, a company pays for what it owns (assets) by borrowing money as a service (liabilities) or taking from the shareholders or investors (equity).

    Structure of a balance sheet

    The balance sheet has three important components: assets, liabilities, and equity.

    Assets

    Assets are resources that a company owns, that help in the positive financial growth of the company. Assets can be of different types like, current and non-current, tangible and intangible, and operating and non-operating. Some examples of assets are short-term deposits, buildings, office supplies, patents, machinery and equipment, and marketable securities. The assets are listed on the left side of the balance sheet.

    Liabilities

    Liabilities are obligations that a company owes to other parties. They are financial commitments, including debts, that a company settles by paying in cash or providing equivalent services to the other party. They can be classified as current and non-current. Examples include short-term loans, accounts payable, and deferred tax. The liabilities are listed on the right side of the balance sheet.

    Equity

    Equity is equal to the total assets minus total liabilities. It is the amount that has to be handed over to the shareholders after paying debts and liquidating assets. Equity represents the net value of the company. If equity is positive, it means the company has enough assets to pay for its liabilities, and if it is negative, the liabilities exceed the assets.

    Calculating net income

    Companies use the income statement to calculate their net income. It is calculated with the formula:

    Net income = (Revenue + Gains) - (Expenses + Losses)

    If the net income is positive, it is known as net profit. If it is negative, it is known as net loss.

    Structure of an income statement

    The income statement always contains revenue, gains, expenses, and losses. However, its format differs based on regulatory requirements, business needs, and the operating activities of the company.

    Revenue

    The first section of the income statement displays revenue, which is classified into operating and non-operating. Operating revenue includes revenue gained by activities like manufacturing or providing services. Non-operating revenue includes non-core business activities like installing, operating, and maintaining systems.

    Gains

    Gains are also called other income. Gains include money realized by the company from various business activities, like the sale of an operating segment or of old assets like vehicles or unused lands.

    Expenses

    Expenses are the costs that companies have to pay to generate revenue. Like revenue, expenses are classified into operating and non-operating. Operating expenses include sales commissions, pension contributions, and payroll, while non-operating expenses include interest paid on loan and lawsuit settlements.

    Losses

    Losses include expenses involved in selling assets for less than what you paid for them, as well as unusual costs and lawsuits.

    What is Gratuity?

    Gratuity is a financial benefit paid by an employer to an employee as a token of appreciation for their services. It is governed by the Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972, and is paid at the time of retirement, resignation, or termination after completed minimum service period.

    Gratuity Formula

    For organisations covered under the Payment of Gratuity Act, gratuity is computed using the following statutory formula:

    Gratuity = (Last Drawn Monthly Salary × 15 × Years of Service) ÷ 26

    Here, the monthly salary is defined as Basic Salary + Dearness Allowance (DA). A standard working month is considered to have 26 days, and the gratuity is calculated at the rate of 15 days of salary per year of completed service.

    Eligibility Criteria

    An employee is eligible to receive gratuity if they satisfy the following criteria:

    • The employee must be eligible for superannuation or retirement.
    • The employee must have resigned after a continuous employment of at least 5 years with the same employer.
    • In the case of death or disablement due to accident or disease, the 5-year minimum service rule is waived.

    Tax Rules for Gratuity

    Gratuity received by employees is subject to specific tax exemptions under the Income Tax Act:

    • Government Employees: Gratuity received by government employees is completely tax-exempt.
    • Non-Government Employees: Tax exemption is allowed up to the least of:
      • Actual gratuity received
      • Eligible gratuity amount calculated using the standard formula
      • Capped limit of ₹20 Lakhs (amended under current rules)

    Examples

    Let's consider two examples of gratuity calculations:

    • Example 1 (Service <= 6 months addition):
      Basic + DA: ₹50,000 | Completed Years: 5 | Additional Months: 5
      Since 5 months is less than or equal to 6, the years considered remain 5 years.
      Gratuity = (₹50,000 × 15 × 5) ÷ 26 = ₹1,44,230.77
    • Example 2 (Service > 6 months addition):
      Basic + DA: ₹50,000 | Completed Years: 5 | Additional Months: 7
      Since 7 months is more than 6, the service years are rounded up to 6 years.
      Gratuity = (₹50,000 × 15 × 6) ÷ 26 = ₹1,73,076.92

    What is per diem?

    "Per diem" (Latin for "by the day") is a daily allowance paid to employees to cover travel expenses like lodging, meals, and incidental expenses. Established by government bodies, per diem rates vary depending on the destination city and state, ensuring employees are fairly compensated for travel costs without requiring complex receipt tracking.

    Lodging

    Covers overnight accommodation costs, including hotels, motels, and service apartments. Taxes are handled separately under corporate expense rules.

    Meals

    Covers breakfast, lunch, dinner, and non-alcoholic beverages during business travel. Rates depend heavily on local restaurant cost indexes.

    Incidental Expenses

    Covers minor costs like luggage tips, porter fees, room service gratuities, and laundry. Rates are fixed at standard national guidelines.

    Per diem regulations established by the government

    In the United States, federal per diem rates are regulated by three main agencies depending on travel destination: the General Services Administration (GSA) establishes rates for the continental US (CONUS); the Department of Defense (DoD) sets rates for non-foreign areas outside CONUS (OCONUS) such as Alaska and Hawaii; and the Department of State (DoS) governs international travel rates.
